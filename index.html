        B1 Beginner · 5 min

        Anatomy of a model rocket

        Every model rocket — from a 30-gram Estes kit to a two-metre high-power build — has the same core parts. Learn them once and every design you see afterwards will make sense.

        A rocket is a pressure vessel with a hole in one end, wrapped in an airframe that keeps it pointed the right way and brings it back in one piece. The airframe has three jobs:

        • Hold the motor and transfer its thrust into the body (motor mount, centering rings, engine hook).
        • Stay aerodynamically stable — nose cone in front, fins at the back.
        • Recover safely — the motor's ejection charge pops the nose off and deploys a parachute or streamer.

        B2 Beginner · 6 min

        Newton's three laws, on the launch pad

        Rockets don't push against the air or the ground. They throw mass out the back very fast — and Newton guarantees the rocket goes the other way.

        First law — inertia

        An object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity, unless a net force acts on it. A rocket on the pad is in balance: its weight pulls down, the pad pushes up. Nothing happens until the motor creates a force bigger than the weight.

        After burnout the rocket keeps climbing — that's inertia. Gravity and drag slowly take that velocity away until the rocket stops at apogee.

        Second law — F = m·a

        Acceleration equals net force divided by mass. For a rocket on the pad, the net force is thrust minus weight:

        a = (T − m·g) / m

        If thrust is less than weight the rocket doesn't move at all. The ratio T / (m·g) is the thrust-to-weight ratio. Hobby guidelines ask for at least 5 : 1 so the rocket leaves the launch rod fast enough to be stable.

        Try the lab: lower the thrust or raise the mass until the rocket can't lift off.

        Third law — action and reaction

        For every action there's an equal and opposite reaction. Burning propellant creates hot, high-pressure gas; the nozzle accelerates it out the back at thousands of metres per second, so the rocket is pushed forward.

        This is why rockets work in a vacuum. There's nothing to "push against" — the rocket pushes against its own exhaust. Thrust is simply:

        T = ṁ · vₑ (+ pressure term)

        where ṁ is mass flow rate and vₑ is exhaust velocity. You'll meet the pressure term again in I6 · Nozzle design.

        B3 Beginner · 5 min

        The four forces of flight

        Thrust, weight, drag and lift act on every rocket. Which one wins changes through the flight — and that decides what the rocket does next.

        Thrust
        Push from the motor, along the rocket's axis. Only exists while the motor burns.
        Weight
        Gravity pulling the total mass down, acting at the centre of gravity. Falls slightly as propellant burns.
        Drag
        Air resistance, always opposite to the direction of motion. Grows with the square of speed.
        Lift
        Sideways force from the fins when the rocket is at an angle to the airflow. It's what corrects the rocket's heading.

        LAB 3.1 · FORCE DIAGRAM BY FLIGHT PHASE

        Key takeaway During boost thrust dominates; during coast weight and drag decelerate the rocket; during descent drag (mostly the parachute) balances weight.

          B5 Beginner · 7 min

          Reading a motor code

          The code printed on every hobby motor — like C6-5 — tells you how much push it has in total, how hard it pushes, and how long it waits before deploying the parachute.

          C6-5
          • Letter — total impulse class (N·s). Each letter doubles the maximum: a C has up to twice the total impulse of a B.
          • Number — average thrust in newtons. Higher = harder kick, shorter burn.
          • Delay — seconds between burnout and ejection. -0 means no ejection (booster stage); P means plugged.

          Since impulse = average thrust × burn time, you can estimate the burn time straight from the code.

          B6 Beginner · 8 min

          Stability: centre of gravity vs. centre of pressure

          The single most important rule in rocketry: the centre of gravity (CG) must sit ahead of the centre of pressure (CP) — by about one to two body diameters.

          CG is the balance point — where the rocket's mass is centred. Find it by balancing the loaded rocket on your finger.

          CP is where the aerodynamic forces are centred. Fins at the back drag the CP rearward, the same way feathers keep an arrow straight.

          When a gust tilts the rocket, air pushes on it at the CP. If the CP is behind the CG, that push rotates the nose back into the wind — the rocket corrects itself. If the CP is in front, the push makes the tilt worse and the rocket tumbles.

          The distance between them, in body diameters (calibers), is the stability margin:

          • < 1 cal — marginal or unstable. Add nose weight or bigger fins.
          • 1 – 2 cal — the sweet spot.
          • > 2.5 cal — over-stable: it weathercocks hard into the wind.

          The safety code & pre-flight checklist

          Hobby rocketry has an excellent safety record because everyone follows the same code. Run the checklist below — the launch button only arms when every item is checked.

          This checklist summarises the key points of the NAR Model Rocket Safety Code. Always read the full code from your national association (NAR, Tripoli, UKRA, etc.) and follow local law.

          • Only certified, commercially made motors. Never modify a motor or mix your own propellant at the beginner level.
          • Ignite only with an electrical launch system with a removable safety key.
          • If a motor misfires: remove the key and wait 60 seconds before approaching.
          • Never try to retrieve a rocket from power lines or tall trees.

            I1 Intermediate · 8 min

            Thrust curves & grain geometry

            Two motors with identical total impulse can fly very differently. The shape of the thrust curve comes from the shape of the propellant grain — because thrust tracks the burning surface area.

            Solid propellant burns perpendicular to its exposed surface at a roughly constant rate. So the thrust over time follows how the burning area changes as the flame front moves:

            • End burner — burns like a cigarette. Constant area → neutral, long and gentle.
            • Core burner — hollow tube burning outward. Area grows → progressive.
            • BATES — short cored segments, ends exposed. Growing core offsets shrinking ends → nearly neutral.
            • Star — star-shaped core. Points burn away fast → high initial thrust, then regressive.

            The area under the curve is the total impulse: I = ∫ T dt. Drag across the chart to read instantaneous thrust and impulse delivered so far.

            I2 Intermediate · 8 min

            The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation

            How much velocity change (Δv) a rocket can produce depends on only two things: how efficient its engine is, and what fraction of it is propellant.

            Δv = Isp · g₀ · ln(m₀ / mf)

            • Isp — specific impulse (seconds); engine efficiency.
            • g₀ — 9.80665 m/s². Isp·g₀ is the effective exhaust velocity.
            • m₀ / mf — mass ratio: fully fuelled mass over empty mass.

            The natural log is the brutal part. Doubling the mass ratio doesn't double Δv — it adds a fixed amount. That's the tyranny of the rocket equation, and the reason orbital rockets are about 90 % propellant.

            Reaching low Earth orbit takes roughly 9.4 km/s of Δv once gravity and drag losses are included. Try to get there with one stage.

            I3 Intermediate · 6 min

            Specific impulse & propellants

            Specific impulse is "miles per gallon" for rockets: how many seconds one unit of propellant weight can produce one unit of thrust. Higher is more efficient.

            Isp = T / (ṁ · g₀) ⇔ vₑ = Isp · g₀

            Isp depends on combustion temperature and the molecular weight of the exhaust: hot, light gas leaves fastest. That's why hydrogen wins on Isp — but it's very low density, so it needs huge tanks.

            Isp is always higher in vacuum, because there is no ambient pressure pushing back on the nozzle exit. Toggle the view to compare.

            I5 Intermediate · 7 min

            Recovery: sizing a parachute

            At terminal velocity, parachute drag exactly balances weight. Solve that balance and you can size a parachute for any rocket.

            m·g = ½ ρ v² Cd A ⇒ v = √(2 m g / (ρ Cd A))

            Target a descent rate of roughly 3.5–6 m/s. Slower drifts a long way downwind; faster risks broken fins.

            Drift distance is roughly wind × (altitude / descent rate). For high flights, dual deployment fixes this: a small drogue at apogee for a fast, controlled fall, then the main chute at low altitude.

            Small, light rockets often use a streamer instead — it adds enough drag to slow the fall without drifting.

            I6 Intermediate · 10 min

            The de Laval nozzle

            A converging–diverging nozzle turns hot, slow, high-pressure gas into a cold, supersonic jet. Its expansion ratio decides how well it matches the outside air.

            Subsonic gas speeds up as the duct narrows. At the narrowest point — the throat — the flow reaches exactly Mach 1. Past the throat something counter-intuitive happens: supersonic gas speeds up as the duct widens.

            The expansion ratio ε = Aexit / Athroat sets the exit Mach number and exit pressure pe. Compare pe to ambient pressure pa:

            • Over-expanded (pe < pa) — the plume pinches inward, with shock diamonds. Severe cases separate from the nozzle wall.
            • Ideal (pe ≈ pa) — maximum thrust for that altitude.
            • Under-expanded (pe > pa) — the plume balloons outward; energy is left on the table.

            That's why vacuum engines have enormous bells, and why sea-level engines look stubby.

            I7 Intermediate · 8 min

            Staging: throwing away the empty tanks

            Staging is the workaround for the rocket equation. Once a tank is empty it's dead weight — so drop it, and the remaining rocket has a fresh, better mass ratio.

            Each stage gets its own rocket equation, and the Δv values add up:

            Δvtotal = Σ Isp,i · g₀ · ln(m0,i / mf,i)

            The structural ratio is the fraction of each stage that isn't propellant — tanks, engines, plumbing. The higher it is, the more staging helps.

            In model rocketry, staging uses a -0 booster motor: when it burns out, hot particles blow forward and ignite the upper-stage motor directly.

            A1 Advanced · 10 min

            Kepler's three laws

            In 1609–1619 Johannes Kepler distilled decades of Tycho Brahe's planetary observations into three rules. Newton later showed they all follow from one inverse-square law of gravity — and they apply just as well to satellites as to planets.

            1. Orbits are ellipses with the central body at one focus — not at the centre. Eccentricity e measures how stretched the ellipse is: 0 is a circle, close to 1 is a long thin ellipse.
            2. Equal areas in equal times. The line from the Sun to the planet sweeps equal areas in equal times, so the planet moves fastest at periapsis (closest point) and slowest at apoapsis (farthest point).
            3. T² ∝ a³. The square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. In years and AU around the Sun, simply T² = a³.

            The general form, which works around any body of gravitational parameter μ = G·M:

            T = 2π · √(a³ / μ)

            The speed ratio follows from the second law: vp / va = (1 + e) / (1 − e).

            A2 Advanced · 10 min

            What an orbit really is: Newton's cannonball

            An orbit is falling around the planet — moving sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath you as quickly as you fall towards it. Newton imagined it as a cannon on a very tall mountain.

            Fire the cannon horizontally. Slow shots fall back to Earth. Faster shots land further away — until, at circular velocity, the shot never lands at all. Faster still, the orbit stretches into an ellipse; at escape velocity it never comes back.

            vcirc = √(μ / r) vesc = √(2μ / r)

            Everything in between is captured by the vis-viva equation, the single most useful formula in orbital mechanics. It gives the speed anywhere on any orbit from just the current radius r and the semi-major axis a:

            v² = μ · (2/r − 1/a)

            For Earth, μ = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s². At the ISS's 400 km altitude, circular velocity is about 7.67 km/s — one lap every 92 minutes. Note that orbits are about sideways speed, not height: getting to 400 km takes far less energy than going 7.67 km/s.

            A3 Advanced · 12 min

            The gravity turn: flying to orbit

            A rocket can't just go up and then turn sideways — it must build 7.8 km/s of horizontal speed while climbing out of the atmosphere. The gravity turn lets gravity do the steering for free.

            The rocket lifts off vertically, then makes a small pitch kick of a few degrees. From then on it thrusts exactly along its velocity vector (prograde) and gravity gradually bends the path over towards horizontal. Because thrust never fights the velocity direction, the airframe sees almost no sideways load while passing through max-Q — the moment of peak aerodynamic pressure.

            Every second spent climbing costs gravity loss (∫ g·sin γ dt, where γ is the flight-path angle). Turning early cuts gravity loss but keeps the rocket low in thick air (more drag loss), or makes it fall back. Turning late wastes Δv fighting gravity. The lab below flies a lumped two-stage launcher (Isp 340 s, 9.6 km/s of Δv) to a 200 km orbit, using the same strategy as the game Kerbal Space Program: burn until apoapsis hits 200 km, coast up, circularise.

            A4 Advanced · 12 min

            Hohmann transfer orbits

            The most fuel-efficient way (for most cases) to move between two circular orbits: two prograde burns and a half-ellipse coast. Walter Hohmann worked it out in 1925 — decades before anyone could fly it.

            The transfer orbit is an ellipse that just touches both circles: periapsis on the inner orbit (r₁), apoapsis on the outer (r₂), so its semi-major axis is a = (r₁ + r₂) / 2.

            1. Burn 1 at r₁, prograde: speed up from circular speed to the transfer orbit's periapsis speed.
            2. Coast half an orbit: t = π √(a³/μ).
            3. Burn 2 at r₂, prograde again: speed up to circular speed there. (You arrive slower than circular — you climbed out of the gravity well.)

            Δv₁ = √(μ/r₁) · (√(2r₂/(r₁+r₂)) − 1)
            Δv₂ = √(μ/r₂) · (1 − √(2r₁/(r₁+r₂)))

            To meet a moving target — a space station, the Moon, Mars — you must leave when the target is the right phase angle ahead, so it arrives at apoapsis at the same moment you do. For Mars that alignment comes round only every ~26 months: a launch window.

            A5 Advanced · 12 min

            Orbital maneuvers: flying in a counter-intuitive world

            In orbit, "speed up" and "go higher" are the same command — but the height changes on the other side of the planet. Get hands-on in the sandbox, then complete the challenges.

            Prograde
            Along your velocity. Raises the opposite side of the orbit. Burn at periapsis to raise apoapsis.
            Retrograde
            Against your velocity. Lowers the opposite side — how spacecraft de-orbit.
            Radial in / out
            Towards or away from the planet. Rotates the orbit around you; rarely efficient.
            Normal / anti-normal
            Perpendicular to the orbital plane. Changes inclination — the most expensive maneuver of all.

            Burns are most efficient where you're moving fastest — deep in the gravity well at periapsis. This is the Oberth effect: the same Δv adds more orbital energy when speed is high, because energy grows with v².

            Plane changes are costly because you must redirect your whole velocity: Δv = 2v · sin(Δi/2). That is why plane changes are done at apoapsis, where v is lowest — and why launching due east from near the equator matters so much.
